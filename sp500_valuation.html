What this answers. Not "is this dear versus its own history" — the pack already does that — but "is this dear versus other stocks, today". Those are different claims with very different evidence behind them: a 2016 CRSP/Compustat study puts cross-sectional value at 5.50%/yr gross long-short (1990–2014) against 1.25%/yr for the own-history version. Cheap is coloured green here because that is the direction the cross-sectional evidence points; the same green on the pack's own-history view is a far weaker claim.

How this is computed, and why it will not exactly equal a vendor's number. Market value is FMP's full market cap, not float-adjusted, while S&P weights the index on float — so this is a full-cap aggregate and runs a little different by construction. Multiple share classes of one issuer are collapsed first (FMP reports Alphabet's entire 70.3T-scale cap under both GOOGL and GOOG); leaving them in doubles that issuer's weight and moved the trailing aggregate by 3.1% on this run. Loss-makers are kept in the earnings sum, which is the honest index number — dropping them always reads cheaper. The pack's Global-equity-valuations section reports a separate S&P 500 trailing P/E from MSCI/Damodaran; this one is computed here from constituents. They are two independent derivations of the same idea and land within a few percent — do not net them against each other, and do not treat a gap between them as a market signal. This one is 26.4x trailing.

The gap has a name. Against IVV's 25.1x, this page reads +5.0% including loss-makers and +0.3% excluding them. The second number is the whole story: published index multiples drop companies that lose money, which always reads cheaper. Our two figures bracket the vendor's rather than disagreeing with it.
Do not net these against each other and do not read a moving gap between them as a market signal. They are independent derivations, and a change in one can be a change of vintage or convention rather than of price. The pack applies the same rule to its own international rows, where crossing a trailing multiple with a forward average invented ~17 points of richness out of the definition alone.
Method. NTM P/E = price ÷ NTM consensus EPS, where NTM EPS blends the consensus FY1 and FY2 annual EPS by the time remaining in FY1: w = days to FY1 end / 365, NTM EPS = w·FY1 + (1−w)·FY2. The blend is not a refinement — fiscal years end in different months, so a raw FY1 multiple compares a company eleven months into its year against one that is one month in, and the ranking would inherit that error.
Sources. Consensus EPS: FMP /stable/analyst-estimates (annual). Price and market cap: FMP /stable/quote. Constituents, company names and GICS sectors: Coverage Manager's cached Wikipedia list. All refreshed by the same Friday job that builds the weekly pack.
What this is not. It is a snapshot of embedded expectations, not a return forecast and not a buy list. A low multiple is frequently correct pricing of a deteriorating business, and consensus EPS is a lagging object — a name that has just guided down can print a flatteringly low multiple until estimates catch up. Cross-sector comparison on P/E alone is unsound, which is why the sector quintile sits beside the market one.
